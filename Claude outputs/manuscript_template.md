## Abstract

Dispersion problems ask for a subset of elements that is as spread out as possible. We study the bi-objective (MaxSum + MaxMin) maximum diversity problem (MDP) and two constrained variants, the capacitated (CDP) and generalised (GDP) dispersion problems, comparing a MOEA/D with a pool of eight operators against NSGA-II, SMS-EMOA, GRASP, variable neighbourhood search (VNS) and tabu search on 443 instances. Scaling the objectives inside the Tchebycheff function is decisive (+0.11 to +0.61 normalised hypervolume). Against baselines with fixed budgets MOEA/D is significantly ahead; when every method is tuned with the same protocol and given the same wall-clock time, it stays ahead on the GDP and, at equal time, on the CDP, but is clearly behind a tabu search, a VNS and a tuned GRASP on the MDP. No adaptive operator selection beats uniform selection from the same pool on instances of up to about 200 elements or on the CDP, whatever the selector (UCB1, Thompson sampling, the published DYTS, FRRMAB), reward, budget or context; with the hypervolume reward the selectors win on 2,000-element instances and on GDP instances with a tight cost budget. A short MaxMin intensification brings the MaxMin end of the front within about 1% of the best published CDP values on four of five instance families and improves the hypervolume. In the CDP and GDP the MaxSum objective grows with the size of the set, so all methods work in the size regime induced by the capacity constraint, which we quantify.

**Keywords:** Metaheuristics; Evolutionary computations; Multiple objective programming; Combinatorial optimization; Dispersion problems

## 1. Introduction

Dispersion and diversity problems arise in the placement of undesirable facilities, workforce management and social media, among others (Peiró et al., 2021); our application-inspired MDP instances mimic sensor, facility, portfolio and team selection. The classical formulations optimise one measure of dispersion: MaxSum (maximise the sum of pairwise distances, the maximum diversity problem), or MaxMin (maximise the minimum pairwise distance, the p-dispersion family). The two measures lead to solutions of very different structure — the MaxSum optimum selects points close to the borders of the space, whereas the MaxMin optimum spreads them evenly over it, central region included (Parreño et al., 2021) — and practitioners often care about both. Two constrained variants add realism: the capacitated dispersion problem (CDP) requires the selected elements to supply a minimum aggregate capacity, and the generalised dispersion problem (GDP) additionally bounds the total opening cost. The bi-objective version of the unconstrained problem, pairing MaxSum with MaxMin, is the *bi-objective diversity problem* of Colmenar et al. (2018), which they attacked with NSGA-II, SPEA2, GRASP, iterated greedy, tabu search and VNS on the 145 GKD instances of MDPLIB (n up to 500, m up to 50), each method tuned with irace (López-Ibáñez et al., 2016); to our knowledge the literature on CDP and GDP, in contrast, is single-objective (MaxMin), and the one multi-objective CDP study we are aware of pairs MaxMin with a colour-symmetry penalty rather than with MaxSum and uses an ε-constraint method and a biased-randomised constructive heuristic (Gomez et al., 2026).

Decomposition-based evolutionary algorithms such as MOEA/D (Zhang and Li, 2007) are natural candidates, and recent work equips them with *adaptive operator selection* (AOS): a bandit chooses, at every step, which variation operator to apply, rewarded by the improvement it produces (DaCosta et al., 2008, dynamic bandits; Li et al., 2014, MOEA/D-FRRMAB, with a sliding window of fitness-improvement rates, rank-based credit assignment and a decaying selection rule; Sun and Li, 2020, dynamic Thompson sampling; Geng et al., 2023, MOEA/D-CTAOS, with a classification tree that predicts the outcome of each operator). The premise is that operators differ in usefulness and that this can be learned online. This paper tests that premise in a controlled setting and asks four questions.

- **RQ1.** Does a MOEA/D framework with a pool of eight operators outperform classical multi-objective heuristics (NSGA-II, GRASP, VNS, tabu search) on bi-objective MDP, CDP and GDP — also when those heuristics are tuned and given the same wall-clock time?
- **RQ2.** Does adaptive selection add value over *uniform* selection from the same pool, and how does that depend on instance size, constraint tightness and budget?
- **RQ3.** How much do the scaling of objectives and the coverage of the Pareto-front ends matter?
- **RQ4.** Which parts of the operator pool matter, and is the pool robust to pruning?

**Contributions.**

- *Protocol.* A replicated, indicator-robust evaluation of the bi-objective MDP, CDP and GDP on 443 benchmark instances, against frozen reference fronts and with three quality indicators, validated against the public single-objective benchmarks. It includes equal-time comparisons with baselines — NSGA-II, SMS-EMOA, GRASP, and a tabu search and a VNS in the style of Colmenar et al. — that are tuned by the same protocol as the proposed MOEA/D.
- *Where MOEA/D stands.* The answer depends on the problem and on how the baselines are treated. MOEA/D is clearly ahead on the GDP and, at equal time, on the CDP, where five times the time lets tuned GRASP, VNS and tabu search catch up; it is behind a tabu search, a VNS and a tuned GRASP on the unconstrained MDP.
- *Objective scaling.* Normalising the objectives inside the Tchebycheff function is the single most important design choice, with an effect that grows with instance size.
- *Adaptive operator selection.* A controlled, replicated study with three bandit families, the published dynamic Thompson sampling and FRRMAB, several reward signals, hyper-parameters, budgets and contexts. Uniform selection from the pool is as good as any selector on instances up to about 200 elements and on the CDP, and a sizeable family of selectors is significantly worse. The HV-reward selectors do win on large instances and on instances with a tight cost budget, with an advantage that depends on the generation budget: a map of when adaptive selection pays off, not a blanket verdict.
- *The pool.* No operator suffices alone and MaxMinSwap cannot be removed, but BiObjBestSwap, BestSwap1×1 and GraspGreedy can be (singly on both problems, together on the CDP) without loss, and the uniform pool also tolerates three deliberately useless operators added to it.
- *Covering the ends of the front.* A short MaxMin intensification (a tabu search followed by a solution-based tabu search) brings the MaxMin end of the front to within about 1% of the best published single-objective values on four of five CDP families and above the published GDP values, and improves the hypervolume; the hardest family (CDP MDG-b) remains at 0.85 of the best published value.
- *A hidden dimension.* In the CDP and GDP the size of the selected set is a hidden dimension of the bi-objective problem, which all methods leave at the size induced by the capacity constraint; we quantify what this leaves unexplored.
- *Open data.* Reference fronts, about 100,000 run files and the analysis scripts that regenerate every table and figure.

## 2. Related work

*Dispersion problems.* Rosenkrantz et al. (2000) introduced dispersion under capacity and cost constraints. The CDP was brought to the maximum-diversity benchmark setting by Peiró et al. (2021), who proposed a GRASP–VND hybrid within strategic oscillation and built the 100-instance testbed from MDPLIB (capacities in [1, 1000], B equal to 0.2 or 0.3 of the total capacity), which is the testbed we use. Martí et al. (2021b) added a node-packing-based model and a scatter-search heuristic; Landete et al. (2023) studied further formulations and valid inequalities; Mladenović et al. (2022b) proposed basic, general and skewed variable neighbourhood search; Lu et al. (2023) a solution-based tabu search (SBTS); and Rosati and Schaerf (2024) a multi-neighbourhood simulated annealing together with a harder dataset from real maps and compact models. Gomez et al. (2022) proposed a multi-start biased-randomised algorithm, and later work added stochastic and learning-based variants (Gomez et al., 2023; Ghorbani et al., 2024). The GDP was introduced by Martínez-Gavara et al. (2021) with a GRASP and a tabu search; Lozano-Osorio et al. (2022) gave a compact and a standard linear formulation and a multi-start basic VNS, tightened the budgets of the benchmark (whose cost constraint was inactive at the optimum) and released instances, code and solutions; Mladenović et al. (2022a) proposed a multiple-neighbourhood tabu search.

*Multi-objective diversity.* Multi-objective diversity has been studied since 2018. Vera et al. (2018) proposed a MOEA for the simultaneous optimisation of up to five diversity measures (Max-Sum, Max-Min, Max-MinSum, Min-Diff, Min-P-center), with fixed and free subset size; Colmenar et al. (2018) defined the bi-objective diversity problem (Max-Sum and Max-Min), compared NSGA-II, SPEA2, GRASP, iterated greedy, tabu search and VNS on the 145 GKD instances of MDPLIB (75 small, 50 medium and 20 large, with n up to 500 and m up to 50), each method tuned with irace on 29 of the instances (at most 500 experiments per algorithm; more experiments gave similar results) and judged by hypervolume (computed against the origin), set coverage and the ε-indicator, following the guidelines of Knowles et al. (2006), with 10 runs per instance. The tabu search was the best method overall: it reached the best hypervolume (ties included) on 109 of the 145 instances (17 of the 20 large ones), the best average ε-indicator and the shortest run times, while GRASP had the highest average hypervolume over all instances (and the best on the medium ones) and NSGA-II the highest set coverage; the tabu search owed its hypervolume to the extreme regions of the front, whereas NSGA-II concentrated on the central part. They are the direct precedent of our MDP experiments. Their study uses the GKD family only, no decomposition, adaptive operator selection or objective scaling, and no constrained variant. The tutorial of Martí and Martínez-Gavara (2023) has a chapter on multi-objective diversity. Exact approaches exist for related bi-objective location–diversity problems: a bi-objective p-median/max-sum diversity problem (Yang et al., 2026) and a bi-criteria p-hub median and dispersion problem (Ramamoorthy et al., 2024). We found no prior work that evaluates evolutionary multi-objective algorithms on the MaxSum + MaxMin versions of the *capacitated* and *generalised* dispersion problems, nor one that studies operator-pool design and adaptive operator selection in the diversity setting; the search covered web and publisher indices on 5 October 2026 `[TODO: repeat the search (Scopus/Web of Science) just before submitting]`.

*Adaptive operator selection in MOEAs.* Bandit-based AOS was analysed by Fialho et al. (2010), who proposed a sliding-window UCB and studied it on artificial reward scenarios and on a genetic algorithm for the Royal Road problem, including its sensitivity to the hyper-parameters, and by DaCosta et al. (2008) (dynamic bandits with a change-point test). In MOEA/D, Li et al. (2014) introduced FRRMAB over differential-evolution operators and reported, on the UF benchmark, that it improves on other AOS strategies and on uniformly random operator selection, which was the worst of the strategies compared; Sun and Li (2020) replaced the bandit by a dynamic Thompson sampler (19 benchmark problems), and Geng et al. (2023) by a classification tree that predicts the outcome of each operator (UF and LZ problems). These studies use continuous test problems and pools of differential-evolution variants. Our setting is a discrete one with a pool of complementary neighbourhood operators; there, uniform selection from the pool is as good as any selector we tried on instances up to about 200 elements, and the circumstances in which selectors win are of a different kind (Section 6.11).

## 3. Problems

Let V = {1,…,n} be a set of elements with symmetric distances d_ij ≥ 0. A solution is a subset S ⊆ V with |S| ≥ 2. We maximise two objectives,

 f₁(S) = Σ_{i<j; i,j∈S} d_ij  (MaxSum),  f₂(S) = min_{i<j; i,j∈S} d_ij  (MaxMin).

**MDP.** |S| = m, no further constraint. **CDP.** Each element has a capacity c_i ≥ 0 and S must satisfy Σ_{i∈S} c_i ≥ B; |S| is free. In the benchmark B = μ·Σ_{i∈V} c_i with μ ∈ {0.2, 0.3} (the suffixes b02 and b03 in the instance names). **GDP.** In addition each element has an opening cost k_i and Σ_{i∈S} k_i ≤ K₁ (constant-cost model; the instance files also carry a second budget K₂ and unit costs of the variable-cost model, which we do not use, in line with the benchmark's own reference implementation). The instance files carry K₁ = ν·Σ_{i∈V} k_i with ν ∈ {0.2, 0.3} (suffixes k02 and k03); Lozano-Osorio et al. tightened the budgets of the earlier benchmark of Martínez-Gavara et al. (2021), whose cost constraint was inactive at the optimum, so that the budget is active here. That the constant-cost reading is the one used for the published solutions is confirmed empirically: all 80 published n = 500 solutions are feasible under it (Section 5).

The original CDP and GDP literature optimises f₂ only, and in the GDP the number of selected elements is not fixed beforehand, which Martínez-Gavara et al. (2021) consider more realistic than the cardinality of the classical MDP. Adding f₁ is our extension; it makes the feasible set's trade-off explicit: small sets raise MaxMin, large sets raise MaxSum. **The size of the set is a hidden dimension of this bi-objective problem.** Since f₁ never decreases when an element is added, any element at distance at least f₂(S) from every member of S can be inserted without lowering f₂ and with a larger f₁, so a solution is dominated by a larger one whenever such an element exists (and, on the GDP, fits in the budget). All methods of the main study construct solutions up to the required capacity, exchange elements one by one and repair only what the constraints demand; none of them chooses the size. The size nevertheless drifts upward, because a swap that breaks the capacity requirement is repaired by adding an element and the replacement rule favours the larger f₁: on the CDP the archive solutions of the MOEA/D end with about twice the number of elements of the smallest feasible solution we can build (Section 6.14). The fronts, the reference fronts and HV_norm of the main study refer to this regime, which the capacity requirement and the repair induce rather than the decision maker, and Sections 6.13 and 6.14 quantify what it leaves unexplored and compare it with two alternatives: operators that choose the size, and a variant with a bound on the cardinality, which has a finite Pareto front. The MDP has a fixed cardinality and is not affected.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 MOEA/D with a pool of operators

We use MOEA/D with N weight vectors λ^i = (λ_i, 1 − λ_i), λ_i equally spaced in [0.001, 0.999], and the Tchebycheff scalarisation for maximisation,

 g(S | λ, z∗) = max_k λ_k (z∗_k − f_k(S)),

where z∗ is the running ideal point. Each subproblem keeps one solution; the T nearest weight vectors form its neighbourhood. At every step a parent is taken from the neighbourhood with probability 0.9 and from the whole population otherwise; one operator is applied; the child replaces every neighbour for which it is not worse (g_child ≤ g_neighbour). An external archive keeps the non-dominated solutions found (duplicates within 10⁻⁹ rejected). Subproblem i is initialised by a weight-guided GRASP construction (restricted candidate list with α = 0.3 on λ₁Δ₁ + λ₂Δ₂, where Δ₁ is the candidate's summed distance to the current set and Δ₂ its minimum distance, in raw units). The population size N, neighbourhood size T and number of generations depend on n (Appendix A).

Algorithm 1 summarises one run (a generation is N steps, one per subproblem).

```
Algorithm 1. MOEA/D with a pool of operators and a selector
Input: instance, N weight vectors, neighbourhood size T, G generations, pool of 8 operators, selector sigma
 1  for i = 1..N: build S_i by weight-guided GRASP and evaluate it
 2  A <- non-dominated set of {S_i};  z∗ <- component-wise maximum, z_min <- component-wise minimum of the objectives
 3  for g = 1..G:
 4    for i in a random permutation of 1..N:
 5      k <- sigma.select()                      (uniform, UCB1, Thompson sampling or Gaussian sampler)
 6      j <- a random member of the neighbourhood of i (probability 0.9) or of the population (0.1)
 7      S' <- operator_k(S_j)  (SubsetCrossover also takes a second parent from the neighbourhood);  S' <- repair(S') if infeasible
 8      r <- reward of S' (HV gain with respect to A, or Tchebycheff improvement over S_j);  sigma.update(k, r)
 9      for each l in the neighbourhood of i: if g(S' | lambda_l, z∗) <= g(S_l | lambda_l, z∗): S_l <- S'
10      update z∗ and z_min;  A <- non-dominated(A + {S'})
11  optional: add the MaxMin anchor solution(s) of Section 4.5 to A
12  return A
```

**Objective normalisation.** MaxSum and MaxMin live on very different scales (≈10⁴–10⁶ against ≈10⁰–10²). With raw objectives the λ₁-term of g dominates for almost every weight vector, even λ = (0.001, 0.999), so the subproblems collapse onto the MaxSum end. In the normalised regime each objective is divided by its running range (z∗_k − z^min_k) inside g and inside every operator that uses g. All reported results use this regime unless stated otherwise; Section 6.1 quantifies the difference against the raw regime used by our earlier catalogue.

### 4.2 The operator pool

The pool has eight operators; after any of them a feasibility repair restores the constraints (Section 4.4):

1. *RandomSwap1×1* — remove a random element, insert a random outside element.
2. *BestSwap1×1* — remove a random element, insert the outside element that maximises MaxSum.
3. *RandomSwap2×2* — remove two random elements, insert two random outside elements.
4. *GraspGreedy* — rebuild the solution from scratch with a GRASP construction on the MaxSum contribution, with α drawn uniformly in [0, 0.3] at every call.
5. *BiObjBestSwap* — remove a random element and insert the outside element that minimises the Tchebycheff value of the child for the weight vector and ideal point of the current subproblem (all outside candidates are evaluated).
6. *MaxMinSwap* — remove the element that causes the smallest internal distance (the MaxMin bottleneck) and insert the outside element with the best contribution to MaxMin.
7. *LargePerturbation* — remove k random elements, k uniform in {3, 4, 5}, and reinsert them with a GRASP construction (α = 0.2).
8. *SubsetCrossover* — keep the elements common to the current solution and a partner drawn from the neighbourhood, shuffle the elements exclusive to either parent and add them until the cardinality m (MDP) or the capacity requirement (CDP, GDP; within the budget) is met.

### 4.3 Operator selection strategies

*Uniform* selection draws one of the eight operators uniformly at random; it has no memory and no parameters. The adaptive strategies are UCB1 (Auer et al., 2002) with a sliding window (window 50, exploration constant c = 0.5), Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933) with Beta posteriors over a sliding window of binary successes, and a Gaussian sliding-window Thompson sampler (GTS) with a normal-inverse-χ² posterior over continuous rewards. (The sampler appears as `dyts_*` in our code and result files because it was first meant to follow the dynamic Thompson sampling of Sun and Li (2020); it is *not* that algorithm, which uses Beta posteriors, a binary reward and a decay threshold C. The original is implemented separately and tested in Section 6.4.) Two historical reward definitions are used: *HV* — the hypervolume gain of the child with respect to the archive (zero if the child is dominated), and *Scal* — the improvement of the child over its parent in the subproblem's Tchebycheff value. This gives six adaptive MOEA/D variants plus Uniform, i.e. seven MOEA/D configurations. Section 6.4 adds two further reward signals, cost-aware scaling and an exploration floor.

*Success criteria and floating-point tolerance.* The Thompson sampler and the DYTS count a child as a success when its reward is positive, and the insert reward of Section 6.4 gives 1 when no archive member weakly dominates the child. The same set of elements evaluated in a different order gives objective values that differ by about 10⁻¹⁵ in relative terms, which happens whenever a child reproduces a member of the archive or of the neighbourhood (typically after SubsetCrossover). We therefore require a gain larger than 10⁻⁹ in relative terms for a success, and treat a point within that tolerance of an archive member as blocked. Our first runs lacked this tolerance: the Thompson variants then spent 0.20–0.24 of their selections on SubsetCrossover (uniform selection: 0.125), mostly on spurious successes. All results of the variants that depend on the criteria — the two Thompson variants of the catalogue, the insert reward, the published DYTS and its binary variants, FRRMAB and the hyper-parameter and contextual variants built on them — were recomputed with the tolerance; the UCB1 and Gaussian-sampler variants, whose rewards are continuous, are unaffected (their runs are identical bit for bit).

### 4.4 Feasibility repair (CDP, GDP)

After any operator, if the solution is infeasible it is repaired in two phases: elements are removed in increasing order of efficiency (capacity per unit cost; capacity alone in CDP) until the budget holds (GDP only), then outside elements are added in decreasing order of efficiency, skipping those that would break the budget, until the capacity requirement is met. A fallback constructive repair guarantees feasibility. The repair is applied identically to all methods that need it.

### 4.5 MaxMin anchoring

To cover the MaxMin end of the front we add an optional final step. A single-objective tabu search starts from the archive's best-MaxMin solution and from farthest-point constructions; each iteration removes one element of a bottleneck pair, rebuilds the solution by repeatedly adding the element farthest from the current set (random choice among the best three with probability 0.3; recently removed elements are tabu for 10–19 iterations), and prunes redundant elements — removing an element never decreases MaxMin, so good solutions are capacity-minimal. In CDP and GDP rebuilding respects the budget. We use 2,000 iterations × 3 restarts (6,000 × 3 for n = 500) and insert the result into the archive. Cost: ≈2 s per run for n ≤ 150.

*Solution-based variant.* Because the published best values on the hardest family (MDG-b) come from tabu searches over whole solutions, we also implemented a solution-based tabu search in the style of Lu et al. (2023) (our own NumPy reimplementation from the paper's description, not the authors' code). From a greedy farthest-point solution it moves at every iteration to the best non-tabu neighbour in the union of three neighbourhoods — add an element, drop an element that keeps the capacity, and a constrained swap that exchanges an element of the bottleneck pair with one of the eight outside elements farthest from the rest of the solution among those that keep capacity (and, in the GDP, the budget) feasible — and a solution is tabu if it was visited before (exact hash set; if every neighbour is tabu the best one is taken). We use 3,000 iterations for n ≤ 150 and 30,000 for n = 500, either alone (`SBTS`; three restarts at n = 500) or as a second step after the tabu anchor, started from the archive's best-MaxMin solution (`combined`; tabu 2,000 × 3 or 6,000 × 3 iterations, then one SBTS run of 3,000 or 30,000 iterations; both results enter the archive).

### 4.6 Baselines

*NSGA-II* (Deb et al., 2002): tournament selection on rank and crowding distance, subset crossover (probability 0.9, same recombination as operator 8), swap mutation (probability 0.1), GRASP initialisation, feasibility repair, and an external archive. *SMS-EMOA* enters only in Section 4.7. *GRASP-MO*: at each iteration a weight vector is drawn from a Dirichlet(1,1) distribution, a solution is built by a GRASP construction (Feo and Resende, 1995) guided by the weighted sum (α = 0.3, respecting capacity and budget), improved by one best-improvement 1×1 swap pass on the same scalarisation, and inserted into a non-dominated archive. *GRASP-PR* adds a final path-relinking phase over five random archive pairs. *VNS-Pareto* is a variable neighbourhood search (Hansen and Mladenović, 2001) over three neighbourhoods of increasing size (a 1-swap, a 2-swap and a 6-element destroy-and-greedy-rebuild) started from archive members, with early stopping after max(50, 0.3 × iterations) iterations without archive change; *VNS-Tchebycheff* runs the same neighbourhoods per weight vector on the Tchebycheff value. The same families of methods (NSGA-II, GRASP, VNS) were used for the bi-objective MDP by Colmenar et al. (2018), but we know of no published bi-objective MaxSum + MaxMin implementation for CDP/GDP, and we did not have access to their code, so all baselines are our own adaptations, with budgets fixed by the rule of Appendix A and no parameter tuning in the main experiment; the NSGA-II configuration that Colmenar et al. obtained with irace (population 340, 28,130 generations, about 10⁷ individuals produced per run) is roughly 500 times larger than ours (80 × 250 at n = 500), and their best method, a tabu search that moves between non-dominated solutions with a hash-based tabu list, enters our comparison in Section 4.7; we use the same instance, repair, normalisation (where applicable) and archive code for all methods.

### 4.7 Trajectory baselines of Colmenar et al. and the tuning protocol

The baselines of Section 4.6 are the ones used in the main experiment, with fixed budgets and no tuning. Because Colmenar et al. (2018) tuned every method with irace (López-Ibáñez et al., 2016) and found a tabu search to be the best one, we added two trajectory methods in their scheme (`bo_trajectory.py`; our reimplementation from the description in their paper, not their code), SMS-EMOA, and a tuning protocol applied to *all* methods, MOEA/D included.

*Tabu search (TS) and VNS with the front as incumbent.* Following the scheme of Duarte et al. (2015) that Colmenar et al. use, the approximation of the Pareto front is the incumbent solution: an iteration explores the neighbourhood of the current solution S with a local search that feeds the archive; if the archive received no insertion there was no improvement, S becomes a random non-tabu member of the archive and the stagnation counter grows. The constructive is a GRASP with restricted candidate list (g ≥ g_min + α(g_max − g_min)) that alternates the objective between constructions (`altbws`) or at every inserted element (`altins`), or is random. The local search (`pr`) hill-climbs MaxMin from S and MaxSum from S with first-improvement swaps (evaluated in batch per removed element, lexicographic in the other objective), then path-relinks the two results with random steps; every traversed solution is offered to the archive. Alternatives are a local search that alternates the objective per call (`alt`) and one that accepts only dominating swaps (`dom`). In the TS a solution is tabu for `tenure` iterations after it is visited or selected (exact Zobrist hash); in the VNS the shake exchanges k random elements, k grows to k_max and returns to 1 when the archive changes. With a time limit the original stopping rule becomes a restart: after `max_no_improve` stagnant iterations (TS) or `restart_after` complete cycles of k (VNS) a new solution is constructed. On the CDP and GDP, swaps that violate capacity or budget are discarded, the MaxMin local search also accepts removing an element when the capacity is still met and the MaxMin improves strictly, redundant elements are pruned at the end (both points enter the archive) and path-relinking repairs infeasible intermediates. The "irace setting" rows use the values of Table 6 of Colmenar et al. (TS: α = 0.94, constructive `altbws`, local search `alt`, tenure 64% of the number of elements — our reading of "tenure configured as a percentage" —, 70 iterations without improvement; VNS: α = 0.19, `altins`, `pr`, k_max = 29.49% of the solution size, seven cycles of k before a restart).

*SMS-EMOA.* For two objectives the steady-state, hypervolume-based SMS-EMOA (Beume et al., 2007) is the natural alternative to NSGA-II. At every step it produces one child with the NSGA-II operators (random parents, subset crossover, swap mutation, repair, GRASP initialisation), adds it to the population and removes, from the last non-dominated front, the individual with the smallest hypervolume contribution (computed on objectives scaled by the range of the population, with the reference point 0.1 below the minimum); the external archive is the same as in NSGA-II.

*Tuning.* Each method is tuned separately for each of six cells (CDP, GDP and MDP; n ≤ 150 and n = 500) by random search with successive halving on eight training instances that are excluded from the test set (seven for the CDP with n = 500): 16 candidate configurations (the default ones plus random draws from the space below); the first stage runs all candidates on two training instances, the second the best six on three more, the third the best two on the last three; the winner has the highest mean HV_norm over the instances it ran on. Every evaluation runs with the wall-clock limit equal to the time the default MOEA/D-Uniform needs on that instance. The spaces are: NSGA-II and SMS-EMOA — population in [20, 400] (log scale), crossover probability in [0.2, 1], mutation probability in [0.02, 0.9] (log), initialisation GRASP or random, GRASP α in [0.05, 0.5]; GRASP — α in [0, 0.8], 0–3 local-search passes, path relinking on or off, 2–20 relinked pairs; TS — α in [0.3, 1], constructive, local search, tenure in [5, 200], stagnation limit in [5, 300]; VNS — α in [0, 1], constructive, local search, k_max in [2, 40], restart limit in [1, 20]; MOEA/D-Uniform — number of weight vectors in [10, 200], neighbourhood size 5–40% of that number, GRASP α of the initialisation in [0.05, 0.8], with the pool of eight operators and uniform selection fixed. The tuning uses about 56 runs per method and cell (336 over the six cells) and is the same for every method; Colmenar et al. capped irace at 500 experiments per algorithm on 29 instances and report that more experiments gave similar results. The default MOEA/D, the one of the main experiment, is also reported.

### 4.8 Size-aware operators, a cardinality bound and trajectory seeding

Three additions, all opt-in (the standard pool and the standard problem are unchanged and reproduce the stored runs bit for bit), address findings of Sections 6.12 and 6.13.

*Size-aware operators (CDP, GDP).* None of the eight operators of Section 4.2 chooses the size of the set: the swaps keep it, the constructions stop at the capacity requirement, and the repair adds elements only when a move breaks the requirement. Two operators that do are added to the pool (Section 6.14). BiObjGrow inserts the outside element that minimises the Tchebycheff value of the current subproblem, evaluating all candidates in one batch (the budget on the GDP and the cardinality bound below are respected, and the operator leaves at least three outside elements so that the swap operators stay applicable). BottleneckShrink removes one endpoint of the closest pair of the set, the one whose removal leaves the larger MaxMin, provided that the capacity requirement still holds; otherwise it falls back to MaxMinSwap.

*Cardinality-bounded variant (CDP, GDP).* Because f₁ grows with the size of the set (Section 3), the bound |S| ≤ ⌈ρ k_ref⌉ makes the bi-objective problem well posed, with a finite trade-off between small and larger sets. k_ref is the size of the solution returned by the efficiency-greedy construction (elements ordered by capacity per unit of cost), which is feasible by construction on the GDP as well, and ρ = 2 (ρ = 1.5 in a sensitivity run). Every method obeys the bound: constructions and the repair remove, one at a time, the member with the smallest contribution to MaxSum among those that can leave without breaking the capacity requirement (falling back to the greedy solution if none can), and the dominating insertions of tabu search and VNS stop at the bound. The instance classes expose it as `set_max_size(ρ)`; with the default (`None`) nothing changes.

*Trajectory seeding.* In the hybrid variants (E20, Section 6.15) a tabu search or VNS with the tuned parameters of Section 4.7 runs for a fraction of the time limit and its archive becomes the initial population of the MOEA/D (each archive member replaces the subproblems for which it has a smaller Tchebycheff value); the MOEA/D then runs for the rest of the time and the result is the non-dominated union. The seeding phase counts in the time limit, so every method has the same wall-clock budget. A scalarised local-search operator (hill-climbing on the Tchebycheff value of the subproblem until a local optimum or ten swaps) was also tried as a ninth pool member.

## 5. Experimental design

**Instances.** MDP: 143 instances from the families SOM-a (50), SOM-b (12), GKD-c (10), MDG-a (10), MDG-b (40, n ≤ 500), MGPO (5) and 16 application-inspired instances (sensor, facility, portfolio and team selection), with 25 ≤ n ≤ 1,000 (MDPLIB 2.0; Martí et al., 2021a). CDP: 100 instances (GKD-b, GKD-c, MDG-b, SOM-a; 40 with n = 50, 20 with n = 150, 40 with n = 500). GDP: 200 instances (same families; 80 with n = 50, 40 with n = 150, 80 with n = 500). All 200 GDP instance files are byte-identical (MD5) to the public benchmark of Lozano-Osorio et al.; the CDP files use the dense-matrix format inherited with the data. MDG-b with n = 2,000 and MDG-c with n = 3,000 are excluded from the main comparison for cost and covered by a five-instance scalability sample (Section 6.10).

**Budgets.** Fixed numbers of generations/iterations that depend on n only (Appendix A), identical across the replicates; MOEA/D variants share one budget, so they are compared at equal generations. Wall-clock time is *not* an outcome measure of the main experiment because runs shared a laptop with other jobs; time-matched comparisons for the baselines are in Section 6.7.

**Replicates and seeds.** Three replicates per instance and method for n = 500 and MDP, five for CDP/GDP with n ≤ 150, each with an independent seed derived deterministically from (instance, method, replicate) by CRC32. The full-catalogue tables (Tables @@t_ranks@@, @@t_means@@, @@t_aos@@ and @@t_indic@@) use the first three replicates of every instance so that all instances carry the same number of replicates; Table @@t_size@@ uses all five for the n ≤ 150 columns. Runs are checkpointed per file together with a hash of the effective configuration, so a configuration change cannot silently reuse stale results.

**Tuned-baseline comparison (Section 6.12).** The test instances of each cell are those of the equal-time experiment of Section 6.7 (CDP and GDP) or, for the MDP, the 52 instances with 100 ≤ n ≤ 150 of the families SOM-a, MDG-a and MDG-b (n ≤ 150) and 22 instances of the families GKD-c and MDG-b with n = 500, minus the training instances (for the CDP with n = 500 these are drawn from outside that sample, for the other cells from inside it); three replicates. For each instance and replicate the target time T is the wall-clock time of the default MOEA/D-Uniform (measured in the same regime; for the MDP in the same experiment), every method runs with the limit T counting its initialisation (and, in the first replicate of the small cells and in half of the first-replicate instances of the large ones, also with 5T); the references are the frozen fronts of Section 5. A few instances have a degenerate reference front (MaxMin = 0 for every previous run); when a method finds a point with MaxMin > 0 there, HV_norm explodes, so instances with HV_norm > 5 in any E11 run are dropped from the corresponding table.

**Quality indicators.** For every instance a fixed reference front R is the non-dominated union of all valid runs of any method on that instance, and a fixed reference point lies 1% below the worst observed value in each objective. We report HV_norm = HV(A)/HV(R) (primary; hypervolume as in Zitzler et al. (2003)), IGD⁺ (Ishibuchi et al., 2015) and additive ε⁺ (Zitzler et al., 2003) computed on objectives normalised by [reference point, ideal point of R]. Using one fixed R, rather than a front pooled from the methods being compared, avoids rank reversals when methods are added or removed; in our data the two give nearly identical rankings (Section 6.7).

**Statistics.** For each instance the indicator is averaged over replicates. We apply the Friedman test over the twelve methods, Nemenyi's critical difference (CD, α = 0.05, q = 3.268 for K = 12) (Demšar, 2006), and — as the primary pairwise criterion — two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction (Holm, 1979) over the 66 pairs. Effect sizes use the Vargha–Delaney Â12 (Vargha and Delaney, 2000) on instance means. "Top group" means: not significantly different from the best-ranked method under Wilcoxon–Holm.

**Implementation and hardware.** Python 3.12 with NumPy; a laptop with an i5-12450H (8 cores, 12 threads), eight worker processes. Hot paths (swap evaluation, GRASP scoring) were vectorised with bit-exact equivalence to the original loops; after the changes, 70 stored runs (the twelve methods on one small instance of each of CDP, GDP and MDP, and 34 variants of the Sections 6.4–6.6 experiments on CDP and GDP) were re-executed from their stored seeds and reproduced bit for bit (`regression_check.py`; repeated after the recomputation of Section 4.3), and a sample of 26 earlier runs of the main experiment (CDP and GDP; n = 50 to 500) had been reproduced exactly before. `[TODO: add the repository DOI]`

**Validation against the literature.** (i) The public GDP benchmark's 80 published n = 500 solutions, re-evaluated with our code, reproduce their reported dispersion and capacity in 80/80 cases and are feasible in 80/80 (their reader truncates site costs to integers, which differs from ours by ≤ 0.4 in total cost). (ii) We compare the MaxMin end of our fronts with the best published CDP value of each instance (the largest of Table 1 of Gomez et al. (2026), the best result in Tables A.1–A.2 of Lu et al. (2023) and, for MDG-b, Table 5 of Rosati and Schaerf (2024), which also reports the VNS of Mladenović et al. (2022b); 100 instances, both demand factors) and with the values published for the GDP benchmark, which come from a 60-s multi-start VNS heuristic and are not best-known values (Section 6.6).

## 6. Results

### 6.1 Objective normalisation is decisive (RQ3)

Our first catalogue ran MOEA/D with raw objectives. Re-running the seven MOEA/D variants with normalisation (all other settings equal, one replicate, normalised hypervolume against a common reference front) improves HV_norm on most instances and by a margin that grows with n: for CDP with n ≤ 150 (60 instances) the mean gain is +0.17 to +0.19 per variant (Uniform: +0.11), improving 48–55 of 60 instances; for GDP with n ≤ 150 (120 instances) +0.12 to +0.20, improving 79–101 of 120; on the 40 CDP and 80 GDP instances with n = 500 +0.40 to +0.46 and +0.50 to +0.61, improving 37–40 of 40 and 75–80 of 80 (Figure 1). The structure of the comparison also changes: in the raw regime GRASP-PR and GRASP-MO were statistically tied with the MOEA/D family on the CDP with n = 500 (ranks 5.3 and 6.2 against 4.4–5.1 for the MOEA/D variants over 40 instances), but they fall out of the top group once the MOEA/D variants are normalised (ranks 8.0 and 8.6 against 3.5–4.7). The competitiveness of GRASP reported in our earlier catalogue was therefore an artefact of the unscaled scalarisation. (The raw-objective runs of the two Thompson variants carry the floating-point artefact of Section 4.3, which is negligible against gains of this size and is left uncorrected.)

### 6.3 Adaptive selection does not beat uniform selection on average (RQ2)

{{T3}}

Table @@t_aos@@ compares each adaptive variant with uniform selection, paired over instances. No variant is significantly better than uniform selection in any of the three problems (largest Â12 is 0.52). Two variants are significantly *worse*, in all three problems: the scalar-reward bandits (UCB-Scal and TS-Scal). The ordering of the HV-reward variants among themselves is within replicate noise. The effect size of the loss for scalar-reward selectors is non-trivial (ΔHV_norm up to −0.08 on GDP, Â12 = 0.32). These averages pool instances of all sizes; Section 6.11 shows that the verdict for the HV-reward selectors depends on instance size, on the tightness of the cost budget and on the generation budget.

*Why.* Direct inspection of operator-selection frequencies (CDP, n = 50, full runs) explains the pattern. Under HV reward the bandits' reward is zero for every operator after roughly the first 20 generations because the population saturates the small front, so UCB1 and Thompson sampling degenerate to near-uniform selection (selection fractions 0.11–0.17 per operator even in the second half of the run) — the HV-reward selectors *are* uniform selection plus overhead. Under scalar reward the selectors do learn, but what they learn is myopic: they converge to MaxMinSwap and BiObjBestSwap (Figure 3: in the normalised CDP runs MaxMinSwap takes 0.51 of the selections of both UCB-Scal and TS-Scal and BiObjBestSwap 0.24 and 0.21; 0.66 for UCB-Scal in the raw regime), the operators that give the largest immediate Tchebycheff gain, and thereby lose the complementarity of the pool (Section 6.5). In the raw-objective regime the absolute-valued rewards also dwarf the exploration term of UCB1, which assumes rewards in [0,1]. The designs in Section 6.4 target these two failure modes and do not rescue the selectors. The Gaussian sampler with scalar reward (GTS-Scal) stays at 0.12–0.13 per operator, i.e. it is effectively uniform, which is why it ties with uniform selection.

### 6.4 Attempts to repair adaptive selection

Since neither the HV reward nor the scalar reward gives a bandit much to learn from (Section 6.3), we tried five further families of designs on the 60 CDP and the 120 GDP instances with n ≤ 150 (three replicates; two relative-scalar variants of the first family on the CDP only), each against uniform selection on the same instances: (a) other reward signals — an *insert* reward (1 if the child enters the non-dominated archive) and the relative Tchebycheff improvement, each with cost-aware scaling or with a 50/50 exploration floor; (b) the dynamic Thompson sampling of Sun and Li (2020) implemented exactly as published (Beta posteriors, binary *replace* reward, decay threshold C ∈ {30, 100, 300}); (c) contextual selection, with one bandit per third of the weight range and/or of the run; (d) a hyper-parameter sweep of the exploration constant and the window length of the UCB1 and Thompson selectors (17 settings); and (e) FRRMAB of Li et al. (2014), the MOEA/D selector with rank-based credit and a decaying bandit, with the published parameters and four others. We also doubled and quadrupled the generations, giving the selector and uniform selection the same budget. Table @@t_aos_sum@@ summarises the outcome and Supplement S1 gives the details.

{{TS}}

No variant is significantly better than uniform selection, in any family and in either problem (the largest Â12 is 0.52), and between none and five variants per family are significantly worse — those with a scalar or relative-scalar reward, which concentrate on MaxMinSwap and BiObjBestSwap and lose the complementarity of the pool, and on the GDP the published DYTS. Three observations matter for the argument. With the insert reward the selectors spread their selections moderately (0.10–0.18 per operator on the CDP), as with the HV reward, because the reward is zero for most children once the front is crowded. Cost-aware scaling did not make the search faster (median run time 2.70 s against 2.65 s without it on the CDP; 7.45 s against 7.71 s on the GDP). FRRMAB with its published parameters is effectively round-robin (0.124–0.127 of the selections per operator on the CDP): its credits are in [0, 1] and the scaling factor C = 5 makes the exploration term dominate; the published DYTS stays moderately spread (0.06–0.26 per operator on the CDP). Two independent runs of uniform selection differ by +0.006 in HV_norm (Â12 0.52, p = 0.23), which calibrates the noise: differences of that size between equivalent methods mean nothing. More generations help uniform selection (CDP +0.022 at ×2 and +0.036 at ×4; GDP +0.015 and +0.045; p_Holm < 0.01), but at the same budget no selector differs significantly from it. ### 6.5 The pool carries the performance (RQ4)

*Single operators and leave-one-out.* We ran MOEA/D with uniform selection, normalised objectives and three replicates (CDP: 60 instances, GDP: 120; n ≤ 150) with one fixed operator and with the pool minus one operator, and compared each of the 16 variants with the full pool (Table @@t_ops@@; Holm over the 16 comparisons). Every operator alone is significantly worse than the pool (p_Holm < 0.01 in all 16 comparisons). The best single operator is RandomSwap1×1 (−0.046 on the CDP, −0.036 on the GDP); the greedy and recombination operators are worst (GraspGreedy −0.29 and −0.41, BestSwap1×1 −0.21 and −0.30, SubsetCrossover −0.20 and −0.40, MaxMinSwap −0.11 and −0.32). The myopic operators are poor alone and useful in the mix. Removing one operator from the pool hurts for only some of them. MaxMinSwap is indispensable in both problems (−0.036 on the CDP, p = 0.001; −0.039 on the GDP, p < 0.0001); on the GDP SubsetCrossover (−0.028, p = 0.0001) and RandomSwap2×2 (−0.027, p = 0.003) also matter; and the other operators can be removed one at a time without a significant loss (the largest non-significant losses are −0.019 on the CDP, for SubsetCrossover, and −0.020 on the GDP, for LargePerturbation).

{{TO}}

*Lean pools.* Dropping BiObjBestSwap, BestSwap1×1 and GraspGreedy in combination (pools of seven, six and five operators; CDP, 60 instances, three replicates) leaves quality statistically equal to the full pool (ΔHV_norm between −0.008 and +0.010, Â12 0.44–0.52, all p_Holm = 1). Pruning saves little time: after vectorising the swap evaluation, BiObjBestSwap is no longer the dominant cost (the pool without it takes 106% of the time of the full pool) and only dropping GraspGreedy saves 14–18%. We keep the full pool: it is robust to pruning of the costly or redundant operators but not of MaxMinSwap.

*A pool with harmful operators.* A natural objection to the finding that uniform selection suffices is that the pool is well curated, and that adaptive selection would earn its keep if some operators were useless. We added three deliberately bad operators to the eight — a random restart (the solution is replaced by a random feasible one), a replacement of half of the elements at random, and a no-operation — and repeated the experiment on the same instances, with uniform selection and with seven selectors (11 operators; Table @@t_poison@@). Uniform selection is not hurt: it gains +0.018 over the clean pool on the CDP (p = 0.084) and +0.002 on the GDP (p = 0.54), although 27% of its effort now goes to the three new operators; the reason is that a child replaces a neighbour only if it is not worse, so a useless operator wastes effort but cannot damage the population, and the cost of the wasted effort is below the noise at the standard budget (doubling the generations gives +0.022; Section 6.4). No selector improves on uniform selection in the extended pool; UCB1 with the scalar reward is significantly worse on both problems (−0.038 and −0.071) and the published DYTS on the GDP (−0.024; p = 0.002). The selectors that learn something avoid the new operators only mildly (UCB1-HV: 0.07 of the selections for each of them against 0.09 under uniform selection), because the reward that would single them out is zero for almost every operator late in the run.

{{TP}}

*Initialisation.* The weight-guided GRASP initialisation (α = 0.3) is not essential: a purely greedy start (α = 0) is as good (CDP +0.009, p = 0.20; GDP −0.006, p = 0.51), and a random start (α = 1) costs nothing on the CDP (−0.002, p = 0.60) but loses 0.051 of HV_norm on the GDP (p = 0.032), where random solutions are further from feasibility.

### 6.6 Covering the ends of the front: MaxMin anchoring and the literature (RQ3)

*The gap.* With the unscaled scalarisation the MaxMin end of our fronts was far from published values: against the best published CDP value of each instance (see Section 5; 20 instances per family, both demand factors) the best MaxMin among all twelve methods (one replicate) was 10.6% (SOM-a, n = 50), 5.2% (GKD-b, n = 50), 9.0% (GKD-b, n = 150), 12.8% (GKD-c, n = 500) and 62.7% (MDG-b, n = 500) below it; against the 80 published GDP solutions (n = 500) the ratios were 0.93–1.05 (GKD-c) and 0.45–0.50 (MDG-b). The published solutions are rarely dominated by our union front (0–7 of 20 per group) but lie in a region our front does not cover: high MaxMin, low MaxSum. Because the hypervolume is dominated by the knee of the front, this gap costs only 2.6–6.8% of hypervolume on average — the indicator hides it.

*Normalisation helps but is not enough.* With normalised objectives (same comparison: best of the twelve methods, first replicate) the CDP ratios to the best published value rise from 0.894 to 0.936 (SOM-a), 0.948 to 0.972 (GKD-b, n = 50), 0.910 to 0.946 (GKD-b, n = 150), 0.872 to 0.905 (GKD-c) and 0.373 to 0.534 (MDG-b); on the GDP n = 500 pilot (five instances per family) the best MaxMin rises to 1.005 (GKD-c) and 0.69 (MDG-b) of the published value. In an earlier check against the Table 1 values of Gomez et al. (ten b02 instances per family), a trivial farthest-point constructive with 21 restarts reached −6.0% (GKD-c) and −40.6% (MDG-b), i.e. the MaxMin end of MOEA/D is only at the level of a simple heuristic.

*Anchoring narrows the gap.* With the tabu anchoring step of Section 4.5 (Figure 4 and Table @@t_anchor@@; uniform selection; the no-anchor reference is the same method without the step, on the same instances and replicates) the MaxMin end of the CDP fronts with n ≤ 150 moves to 1.000 of the best published value on SOM-a (20 of 20 instances reach it), 0.997 on GKD-b with n = 50 (14 of 20) and 0.984 with n = 150 (0 of 20), from 0.736, 0.935 and 0.929 (both demand factors pooled), and HV_norm rises by +0.019 (better on 39 of 60 instances; Wilcoxon p = 0.015) at a cost of about two seconds per run. On the GDP (120 instances with n ≤ 150), which adds the budget constraint, the same step raises HV_norm by +0.033 (better on 76 of 120; p = 0.0009) at a median extra cost of 0.9 s. At n = 500 (6,000 iterations × 3 restarts) the effect is larger. On the CDP HV_norm rises by +0.073 (better on 37 of 40; p = 3·10⁻¹⁰) and the MaxMin end moves from 0.865 to 0.972 of the best published value on GKD-c and from 0.473 to 0.697 on MDG-b (20 instances each; none reaches it); on the GDP HV_norm rises by +0.052 (better on 61 of 80; p = 3·10⁻⁷) and the MaxMin end moves from 0.975 to 1.094 of the published value on GKD-c (at or above it on 30 of 40 instances) and from 0.592 to 0.917 on MDG-b (22 of 40). The step adds about 20 s to runs of 100–140 s. The published CDP values are the best of 40 runs of C++ solvers (the tabu search SBTS of Lu et al. (2023); for MDG-b also the VNS of Mladenović et al. (2022b) and the simulated annealing of Rosati and Schaerf (2024)); against the mean of the SBTS runs the anchored fronts reach 0.992 (GKD-c) and 0.911 (MDG-b) of the 10-s mean and 0.977 and 0.771 of the 300-s mean, and on MDG-b 0.705 of the best 300-s mean among the three solvers (Xeon 2.1 GHz and C++ against our Python implementation: indications, not an equal-time comparison). Adding the anchor point to *every* method's front, or only to the MOEA/D variants, leaves the ranking between methods unchanged. Anchoring is therefore a coverage and competitiveness device, not a change to the comparison.

### 6.7 Robustness of the comparison to the evaluation protocol

*Budget fairness: baselines at equal wall-clock time.* Generation- and iteration-based budgets favour methods that are cheap per step, so we repeated the comparison with equal wall-clock time (Table @@t_equal@@). For every (instance, replicate) the normalised MOEA/D-Uniform was run first in a worker process and its elapsed time T measured; NSGA-II, VNS-Pareto (without early stopping), VNS-Tchebycheff and the two GRASP variants were then run in the same process with a time limit of T (their initialisation time is not counted, which favours them). The sample is every instance with n ≤ 150 (CDP: 60; GDP: 120) and a subset of the n = 500 instances (CDP: 20, half with each demand factor; GDP: 40, half with the tightest and half with the loosest combination of demand and cost factors, 0.2/0.2 and 0.3/0.3), with three replicates. The median limits are 2.3 s and 67 s on CDP and 3.4 s and 92 s on GDP (the n ≤ 150 group mixes n = 50 and n = 150, so its limits are short). Extra time improves every baseline over its default budget (by +0.01 to +0.12 HV_norm) but, with one exception, does not close the gap: NSGA-II, the two VNS variants and, on GDP and at n = 500, GRASP-MO and GRASP-PR remain significantly behind MOEA/D-Uniform (Holm p < 10⁻⁵ in all those cases). The exception is CDP with n ≤ 150, where GRASP-PR and GRASP-MO at equal time are statistically tied with MOEA/D-Uniform (HV_norm 0.705 and 0.693 against 0.711; Â12 0.43 and 0.40; p_Holm = 0.61 and 0.29 over 60 instances). An earlier pilot in the raw regime with six CDP instances and budgets scaled to the recorded MOEA/D time gave the same picture for NSGA-II and VNS (ranks 10–12 of 12 before and after). ### 6.9 Why GRASP behaves differently on CDP and GDP

On the raw-objective catalogue, GRASP-MO/PR were tied with MOEA/D on CDP but fell to the middle tier on GDP, and NSGA-II moved in the opposite direction; with normalised objectives the GRASP variants are behind MOEA/D on both problems (Table @@t_ranks@@), but the CDP/GDP contrast in how far behind persists (CDP: GRASP-PR 7.75; GDP: 8.91) and so does the contrast for NSGA-II (CDP 10.52; GDP 9.39). To test whether the GDP budget constraint explains it, we re-ran all twelve methods in the normalised regime with the budget relaxed to infinity (leaving only the capacity constraint) on the 120 GDP instances with n ≤ 150 and on twenty instances with n = 500 (ten with the tightest and ten with the loosest combination of demand and cost factors), three replicates each and the same seeds as the original runs, and compared the ranks of the twelve methods within each regime (each regime has its own reference front, because relaxed solutions may violate the original budget). Relaxing the budget improves GRASP-PR (average rank 8.93 → 7.57) and GRASP-MO (9.32 → 8.29) significantly (Wilcoxon on per-instance ranks, Holm p < 0.0001), worsens NSGA-II (9.20 → 10.29) and VNS-Pareto (10.55 → 11.56) significantly (p < 0.0001), and moves the MOEA/D variants by less than 0.8 ranks (UCB-Scal improves by 0.76, p = 0.010; none of the others is significant, the largest change being GTS-HV +0.58, p = 0.11). The budget constraint is therefore a major driver of how GRASP and NSGA-II compare with the MOEA/D family on GDP, but the reversal is partial: relaxed GRASP-PR (7.57) remains four ranks behind the best method (3.48; CD = 1.52). On the twenty n = 500 instances no change is significant (little power at N = 20; GRASP-PR 8.60 → 8.40, NSGA-II 9.55 → 9.75); the one notable movement is that GTS-HV loses 1.90 ranks when the cost budget is relaxed (2.65 → 4.55, p_Holm = 0.065), which fits the finding of Section 6.11 that its advantage over uniform selection is tied to the tight cost budget.

### 6.11 When adaptive selection helps: instance size, constraint tightness and budget

The averages of Section 6.3 pool instances of all sizes. Splitting the per-instance difference between each HV-reward selector and uniform selection by size (Figure 5; fixed reference front, mean over three replicates, descriptive 95% bootstrap intervals over instances) reveals a size effect.

![Figure 5. Difference in HV_norm between the HV-reward selectors and uniform selection, by problem and instance size (mean over instances of the mean over three replicates; bars: 95% bootstrap intervals over instances; fixed reference fronts, except n = 2,000, whose reference is the union of the scalability and budget-regime runs). MDP: no difference up to 200 elements, a growing advantage beyond. GDP: advantage only with n = 500 and the tight cost budget. CDP: none.](figures/fig5_adaptive_selection_vs_size.png)

In the MDP the difference is zero up to 200 elements (n ≤ 50: UCB-HV +0.001, GTS-HV +0.003, N = 24; 51–200: −0.009 and −0.002, N = 80), positive at 201–500 elements (+0.020 and +0.025, N = 34; Wilcoxon p = 0.075 and 0.009, uncorrected), larger at 501–1,000 (+0.031 and +0.049, N = 5, MGPO only) and largest at 2,000 (MDG-b, N = 10: +0.149 and +0.129). In the CDP it stays at zero up to n = 500 (−0.004 and +0.001, N = 40). In the GDP it is ≤ 0 up to n = 150 and positive at n = 500 only on the 40 instances with the tight cost budget (factor 0.2: +0.028 and +0.054, p = 0.075 and 0.0009 uncorrected), not on the 40 with the loose one (factor 0.3: −0.015 and +0.001). The p-values in this paragraph are uncorrected for the number of size groups and are exploratory; the experiment below is the confirmatory test.

*Budget.* If the effect comes from searches that are far from convergence, it should grow as the generation budget shrinks. We changed the number of generations while holding the instances fixed (three replicates, the same seeds as the 1× runs): ¼, ½ and 1× the generations of Appendix A on the 40 CDP, the 40 tight-budget GDP and the 40 loose-budget GDP instances with n = 500, and 1× on ten MDG-b instances with n = 2,000 plus 4× on three of them (Table @@t_budget@@). On the tight-budget GDP the advantage of the HV-reward selectors is monotone in the budget: UCB-HV +0.059, +0.035 and +0.028 and GTS-HV +0.067, +0.059 and +0.054 at ¼, ½ and 1× (Holm p ≤ 0.024 for five of the six comparisons; IGD⁺ gives the same ordering). On the loose-budget GDP and on the CDP there is no effect at any budget (the largest difference is +0.025, GTS-HV on the CDP at ¼; no comparison is significant), although the selectors there concentrate on the same two operators as elsewhere (0.66–0.68 of the selections at ¼): concentration alone does not pay. On MDG-b with n = 2,000 both selectors beat uniform selection on all ten instances at the standard budget (p_Holm = 0.004, the smallest value the test allows with ten instances; IGD⁺: +0.057 and +0.050, ten of ten) and the advantage shrinks but does not vanish with four times the generations (three instances: UCB-HV +0.139 → +0.112, GTS-HV +0.129 → +0.084).

{{T8}}

*Why the tight cost budget matters.* Our first hypothesis was that, with a tight budget, the repair step discards many children of the operators that ignore the cost, so that the success rates of the operators differ more than with a loose budget and there is more to learn. We tested it by re-running 540 standard-budget runs (ten instances in each of three groups at n = 500, and in three at n = 150; same seeds, identical fronts) with per-operator counters (Supplement S3). The first half of the hypothesis holds: on the tight-budget GDP 34% of all children are infeasible before the repair at n = 500 (45% at n = 150), against 0.3% on the loose-budget GDP and 0.7% on the CDP, and the six cost-blind operators produce between 34% and 60% infeasible children. The second half does not: BiObjBestSwap and MaxMinSwap insert 0.07–0.11 children per application into the archive and the other six at most 0.02 in every group, and the dispersion of the insertion rates across operators is not larger under the tight budget (standard deviation 0.037, against 0.035 on the loose GDP and 0.038 on the CDP). What differs is how far the search is from convergence at the standard budget: uniform selection gains 0.139 in HV_norm when its budget grows from a quarter to the standard one on the tight GDP, against 0.068 on the loose GDP and 0.058 on the CDP (n = 500, 40 instances each). A plausible chain is therefore that the tight budget makes a third of the children need repair, progress per generation slows, the search is far from convergence at the standard budget, and moving effort from the six weak operators to the two productive ones pays — which is what the HV-reward selectors do (0.57 of the selections of UCB-HV on the tight GDP, against 0.47 and 0.51). The evidence is at the level of the three instance groups: across instances within a group the advantage of the selectors is not correlated with the convergence slope (|ρ| ≤ 0.22, p ≥ 0.16) and across the 120 instances the correlation is weak (ρ = +0.14, p = 0.13 for GTS-HV), so the mechanism is established for groups and open for individual instances.

*Reading.* Adaptive selection with the HV reward gains where the search is short of convergence and the instance is either large (MDG-b with 2,000 elements) or has a tight constraint to repair (GDP with the 0.2 cost factor); it does not where the reward saturates early (CDP at the sizes tested, every problem below 200 elements) or where the constraint is loose. Budget scarcity explains the dose–response on the tight GDP and the shrinkage at n = 2,000 but not, by itself, the absence of an effect on the CDP with a quarter of the generations, nor on the loose GDP, whose convergence slopes are similar to the CDP's; the measured slope on the tight GDP is twice as large. The verdict of Section 6.3 stands for the regime in which the earlier benchmarks of this literature live (n ≤ 200) and for the CDP; it does not extend to large or tightly constrained instances.

### 6.13 The size of the set as a hidden dimension (CDP and GDP)

Section 3 noted that f₁ never decreases when an element is added. To measure what the capacity-driven regime leaves unexplored we ran the tuned tabu search of Section 6.12 with one more move in its MaxSum local search — insert an element that does not lower MaxMin and fits the budget — on 12 test instances of each of the four CDP and GDP cells, with the same time limit. On the CDP with n ≤ 150 the insertions raise HV_norm from 0.54 to 1.35 (better on 12 of 12 instances), the largest MaxSum found from 0.68 to 5.5 times that of the reference front, and the median size of the archive solutions from 17 to 28 elements. On the GDP with n ≤ 150 the gain is smaller (0.71 to 0.84; better on 7 of 12) and the sizes hardly change (18 to 19 elements) because the cost budget binds. With n = 500 the move changes the hypervolume little (CDP 0.69 and 0.68, GDP 0.61 and 0.62), presumably because within the time limit the search does not reach the region where growth pays, although on the CDP the largest MaxSum rises to eight times the reference value and some solutions contain all 500 elements (for which MaxMin is the smallest distance of the instance). The fronts, the reference fronts and the HV_norm of Sections 6.1–6.12 therefore refer to the size regime that the capacity requirement induces, not to the full Pareto front of the problem as stated: rankings among methods that work in that regime should be unaffected, but the absolute values are not fractions of the true front, and a decision maker who cares about larger sets would face an essentially unbounded front. A formulation with a cardinality bound, or with the average instead of the sum of distances, would be a different problem; we leave it open.

### 6.14 Operators that choose the size of the set, and a cardinality-bounded variant (CDP and GDP)

Section 6.13 showed that the capacity-driven regime leaves the size dimension unexplored. We tested two answers (Section 4.8) on the test instances of Section 6.12 with three replicates and the same wall-clock limit for every method (that of the default MOEA/D-Uniform, initialisation included). First, the pool is extended with BiObjGrow and BottleneckShrink; second, the problem is bounded, |S| ≤ ⌈ρ k_ref⌉ with ρ = 2, which makes the front finite. HV_norm in this section is computed against the reference front built from all runs of each experiment, because the front of the free problem (and of the bounded one) lies outside the frozen reference of Sections 6.1–6.12; values are therefore comparable within a column of Tables @@t_free@@ and @@t_bound@@ and not with other sections. Tuned versions of the MOEA/D, the tabu search and the VNS (Section 4.7) are included; the latter two get the dominating-insertion move of Section 6.13 as an option.

*Where the size of the set ends up.* In the main study the size is not chosen but drifts: the median archive solution of the default MOEA/D has 2.1 times (CDP, n ≤ 150) and 2.0 times (CDP, n = 500) the number of elements of the smallest feasible solution that we can build (k_ref), and 1.3 and 1.2 times on the GDP, where the budget limits the growth.

*Free cardinality* (Table @@t_free@@, Figure 6). Adding the two operators raises HV_norm of the uniform MOEA/D from 0.29 to 0.85 (CDP, n ≤ 150), from 0.59 to 0.81 (GDP), from 0.13 to 0.47 (CDP, n = 500) and from 0.37 to 0.73 (GDP, n = 500), and it ranks first or second in the small cells and third, behind the two hypervolume-reward selectors discussed below, in the large ones (every difference to the default MOEA/D has p_Holm < 10⁻⁴ and A₁₂ ≥ 0.81); the largest MaxSum found is 6.1 times that of the default MOEA/D on the CDP with n ≤ 150, and some archive solutions of the selectors contain 497 of the 500 elements. Tuning the MOEA/D does not matter (0.29 against 0.29; differences between tuned and default are not significant). The tabu search and the VNS benefit from insertions on the small cells (CDP: 0.21 to 0.40 and 0.26 to 0.53) but stay below the MOEA/D with the size operators; on the GDP they only match the default MOEA/D, and with n = 500 the insertion move is hardly reached within the limit (the VNS with and without insertions are the same on the CDP). That the move matters so much shows how far the regime of Sections 6.1–6.12 is from the free problem, and the MOEA/D, with a weight vector per trade-off, is the method that exploits it best.

*Cardinality bounded* (Table @@t_bound@@). With ρ = 2 the methods reach the limit on the CDP (the largest MaxSum of every method is within 10% of that of the plain MOEA/D, and the median size of the archive is 2 k_ref), whereas on the GDP the cost budget keeps the median size at 1.2–1.5 k_ref; the problem becomes a trade-off between small and larger sets within a fixed range and the differences shrink. The MOEA/D with the size operators is still at least as good as the plain MOEA/D everywhere (CDP: +0.05, p_Holm = 0.003, with n ≤ 150, +0.06 (p_Holm = 0.40) with n = 500; GDP: +0.20 and +0.36, p_Holm < 10⁻⁴) and the VNS with insertions matches it on the CDP with n ≤ 150 (0.86 against 0.90; ranks 4.0 and 4.4; difference to the plain MOEA/D not significant), but at n = 500 the trajectory methods and NSGA-II and GRASP are far behind (CDP 0.34–0.58; GDP 0.12–0.25 against 0.37 of the plain MOEA/D and 0.73 with the size operators). The ordering of Section 6.12 therefore survives the bound at ρ = 2 on the GDP and on the CDP with n = 500, and becomes a tie with the VNS (with insertions) on the CDP with n ≤ 150; the next paragraph shows that the tie is not robust to ρ.

*Sensitivity to the bound.* With the tighter bound ρ = 1.5 (cells with n ≤ 150 only) the CDP changes sides: the VNS, with and without insertions, and the tabu search with insertions are the three best methods (HV_norm 0.94, 0.93 and 0.91 against 0.86 for the MOEA/D with size operators and 0.85 for the plain MOEA/D; differences to the plain MOEA/D p_Holm < 10⁻⁴ for the VNS variants and the tabu search with insertions), whereas on the GDP the MOEA/D with size operators stays first (0.84 against 0.70 for the best trajectory method; +0.15 over the plain MOEA/D, p_Holm < 10⁻⁴) and the selectors do not help (CDP: ±0.01, not significant; GDP: −0.04 and −0.02, p_Holm < 10⁻⁴). So on the CDP the answer to RQ1 depends on how much room the bound leaves: with a tight bound the problem approaches the fixed-cardinality MDP, where the trajectory methods of Section 6.12 are strong, and with ρ = 2 or in the free problem the MOEA/D with size operators wins or ties; on the GDP, where a second constraint has to be repaired, the MOEA/D wins at both values. We did not run ρ = 1.5 with n = 500.

*Adaptive selection returns, where it can.* The size operators are worth very different amounts, and this changes the answer to RQ2. On the large cells of the free problem the hypervolume-reward selectors beat uniform selection from the same ten operators (CDP, n = 500: +0.23 for UCB-HV and +0.26 for GTS-HV, better on 19 and 20 of 20 instances; GDP, n = 500: +0.02, p_Holm = 0.09, and +0.05, p_Holm < 10⁻⁴) and, bounded, GTS-HV keeps a smaller advantage (CDP +0.02, p_Holm = 0.008; GDP +0.03 and +0.05; UCB-HV on the CDP is not different). On the small cells the same selectors lose (free CDP: −0.21 and −0.19; GDP: −0.16 and −0.12; bounded: −0.03/−0.02 and −0.14/−0.10; all p_Holm < 0.01). Together with Section 6.11 the picture is consistent: a selector pays when the search is far from convergence at the standard budget (n = 500, 2,000) and the pool contains operators whose value differs by a large factor; it does not pay when the budget is long relative to the instance, even if the pool is heterogeneous. On the small cells the selectors give BiObjGrow 0.39–0.40 of the selections on the free CDP (0.10 under uniform selection) and 0.09–0.19 elsewhere: they over-select the most valuable operator and starve the others, which costs more than it gains when the search converges anyway. A probe on 10 instances of each large cell (first replicate, same limit and seed) shows the same concentration there: BiObjGrow gets 0.35–0.38 of the selections on the free CDP with n = 500 and 0.24–0.26 on the free GDP (0.10 under uniform selection), and on the bounded problem BiObjBestSwap and MaxMinSwap take the lead on the CDP (0.22–0.23 and 0.13–0.14). The share of the most valuable operator is therefore similar where the selectors lose and where they win, so what separates the two regimes is not how much the bandit concentrates but whether concentrating is worth the lost diversity at that budget.

**Table notes and limits.** HV_norm against the reference of the experiment can exceed what a method would obtain against the frozen reference; the bound is a choice and the CDP ranking depends on it (previous paragraph); the dominating-insertion move of the tabu search and the VNS is simpler than BiObjGrow, so the ranking among methods with insertions is design dependent; and the conclusions for n = 500 rest on 20 (CDP) and 32 (GDP) test instances.

### 6.15 Seeding the MOEA/D with a trajectory search

Section 6.12 left one clear weakness: on the MDP the tuned tabu search, VNS and GRASP are ahead of the MOEA/D by 0.09–0.14 in HV_norm, because the front has one to four points and the two ends have to be found by an intensified single-objective search. We tried two remedies on the test instances of Section 6.12, with three replicates, the same wall-clock limit for every method (that of the default MOEA/D-Uniform) and the frozen reference. A scalarised local search (hill-climbing on the Tchebycheff value of the subproblem until a local optimum or ten swaps), added to the pool as a ninth operator, did not help (MDP, 100 ≤ n ≤ 150: 0.80 against 0.83, p_Holm = 0.06): each application costs several swap evaluations and the number of generations falls. Seeding did (Section 4.8): the tuned VNS runs for a fraction φ of the limit and its archive becomes the initial population of the MOEA/D, which then uses the rest of the time (Table @@t_hybrid@@).

On the MDP the gain grows with φ. For 100 ≤ n ≤ 150 HV_norm is 0.91, 0.96 and 0.97 for φ = 10%, 30% and 50% against 0.83 for the default MOEA/D (differences +0.08, +0.13, +0.14; p_Holm < 10⁻⁴ for the three), and with φ = 50% the hybrid is not different from the VNS alone (0.971 against 0.977, p = 0.37); with n = 500 the values are 0.92, 0.96 and 0.97 against 0.89 and 0.996 for the VNS (the hybrid with φ = 50% is still 0.024 below the VNS, p < 0.001). Seeding with the tabu search for 30% of the time gives 0.93 on the smaller instances. On the constrained problems seeding costs nothing: on the CDP with n ≤ 150 the differences to the default MOEA/D are −0.003, +0.009 and +0.030 (p_Holm = 1, 1, 0.13) and the hybrid with φ = 50% equals the tuned MOEA/D (0.735 against 0.736); on the GDP they are +0.004, +0.015 and +0.013 (not significant), and the hybrid is 0.017–0.029 above the tuned MOEA/D (p = 0.066, 0.007, 0.019 without correction). `[TODO: CDP and GDP with n = 500 (queue 11), then restate this paragraph]` The hybrid with φ = 50% is therefore as good as the better of the MOEA/D and the VNS in three of the four cells, closes about 95% of the gap on the MDP with n ≤ 150, and does not damage the problems where the MOEA/D is the better method. Two caveats: φ was not tuned beyond three values, and the trajectory phase uses the parameters tuned for the same cell (Section 4.7), so the hybrid inherits the tuning budget of the VNS as well as of the MOEA/D. An earlier run of the constrained cells, made while another job was using the machine, suggested that seeding hurt there (−0.05 to −0.08); the clean run does not confirm it, a reminder that wall-clock comparisons depend on the load; the runs of this section used the same number of workers (eight) as those of Section 6.12 and the queue waited until no other job was using the machine.

## 7. Discussion

**Where MOEA/D stands.** The answer to RQ1 depends on the problem and on how the baselines are treated. With fixed budgets and no tuning (Sections 6.2 and 6.7) the MOEA/D variants are four to six ranks ahead of NSGA-II, GRASP and VNS everywhere; with a tuning of the same size for every method and the same wall-clock time (Section 6.12) the advantage survives on the GDP and, at equal time, on the CDP, disappears with five times the time on the CDP, and reverses on the unconstrained MDP, where a tabu search or a VNS with the front as incumbent and shake-and-local-search cycles is better. Decomposition with a repair step and a pool of operators pays where there are side constraints to satisfy while exploring; where there are none, a good single-objective search applied to both ends of the front is hard to beat — the same reason that made the MaxMin anchor of Section 6.6 useful. This agrees with Colmenar et al., who found a tabu search to be the best method for the MDP. The hypervolume-based SMS-EMOA, added as a further baseline, behaves like NSGA-II: tuned, it ends 0.11–0.37 below the tuned MOEA/D in every cell (Section 6.12), so the conclusion does not hinge on the choice of the Pareto-based MOEA.

**Where the performance comes from.** Taken together, Sections 6.3–6.5 say that on small and medium instances the benefit of the MOEA/D framework is the *pool*: eight complementary operators mixed at random inside a decomposition that scales its objectives. Choosing among the operators online adds nothing measurable there, and the most sophisticated selectors (scalar-reward bandits) subtract. We see two reasons. The operators act on different aspects of a solution (global restart, bottleneck repair, recombination, perturbation), and their value depends on the state of the subproblem and on the other operators; uniform mixing keeps all of them alive, while any reward-greedy policy concentrates on whichever operator looks best now. The ablations make "complementary" precise: no operator suffices alone, MaxMinSwap cannot be removed (on the GDP neither can SubsetCrossover and RandomSwap2×2), the other operators are individually dispensable, and the uniform pool is not damaged by three deliberately useless ones. The last point is a property of elitist replacement: a child replaces a neighbour only if it is not worse, so a useless operator wastes effort but cannot harm the population, and at this budget the wasted effort costs less than the noise. The second reason is that the reward signals available in a bi-objective archive are weak or myopic: hypervolume gain saturates early, scalarised improvement favours local moves. Section 6.11 qualifies this: where the search is far from convergence (2,000 elements) or the constraint is tight (GDP cost factor 0.2), the hypervolume reward does not saturate in time and concentrating on BiObjBestSwap and MaxMinSwap pays, by up to +0.15 normalised hypervolume. The tight cost budget is the case in which a third of the children need repair and the search is farthest from convergence at the standard budget (Section 6.11), which is why reallocating effort to the two productive operators pays there; we could show this for groups of instances, not for individual instances. So the pool and the selector are not alternatives: the pool provides the diversity, and a selector earns its keep when the budget is short relative to what the search has to do. This does not show that adaptive selection cannot help in other pools or with other policies; it shows where the standard bandit recipes do and do not.

**Implications for practice.** (i) Scale the objectives inside the scalarisation before anything else; the effect grows with n and can reverse conclusions about baselines. (ii) Use a diverse pool with uniform selection as the default for small and medium instances; for instances with thousands of elements or with tight constraints, and for short budgets, test an HV-reward bandit against that default under replication — it may be worth tens of percent of hypervolume. (iii) Check the ends of the front explicitly; HV-type indicators can hide a large MaxMin gap, and a cheap single-objective intensification narrows it. (iv) Tune the baselines with the same protocol as the proposed method before claiming an advantage: it changed the answer on the MDP and removed the advantage at five times the time on the CDP. (v) For the unconstrained MDP use a tabu search or VNS with the front as incumbent; keep the MOEA/D framework for the capacity- and budget-constrained variants. (vi) State the size regime of the constrained bi-objective problems: without a cardinality bound the MaxSum objective rewards growth. (vii) Compare objective values with a relative tolerance in any success-based reward or counter: the same set of elements evaluated in another order differs by about 10⁻¹⁵, which silently turned duplicates into successes in our first runs and distorted the selectors that count successes.

**Limitations.** Budgets of the main experiment are fixed by generations, not by time, and its baselines are untuned and have small budgets; Section 6.12 repeats the comparison with tuned baselines and with the tabu search and VNS of Colmenar et al. (2018), who tuned with up to 500 experiments per algorithm (our tuning: about 56 runs per method and cell, eight training instances), and our tabu search and VNS are reimplementations from their description, not their code (none exists for CDP/GDP to our knowledge); the fronts of the CDP and GDP refer to the size regime that the capacity constraint induces (Section 6.13), so HV_norm is not a fraction of the true Pareto front; replicate counts are three (five for small instances) and the largest instances (MDG-b n = 2000, MDG-c n = 3000) are covered by a five-instance sample only; the budget-relaxation analysis covers GDP only and n = 500 with twenty instances, and the time-matched comparison covers every instance with n ≤ 150 but only 20 CDP and 40 GDP instances with n = 500 (the tuned-baseline comparison uses 20 and 32, and the MDP instances with 100 ≤ n ≤ 150 and with n = 500 of the GKD-c and MDG-b families only); the study uses one hardware platform; no indicator-based or other decomposition-based MOEA besides SMS-EMOA was tested; the mechanism behind the effect of the tight cost budget is established for groups of instances only; the raw-objective catalogue of Section 6.1 was not recomputed after the correction of Section 4.3 and its two Thompson variants carry the artefact; the instance families are the standard MDPLIB/CDP/GDP benchmarks and may not represent all dispersion applications; the reference front depends on the runs included (it is frozen at submission). Selection policies richer than the ones we tested (for instance, contexts learned from the solution rather than fixed by the weight vector and the clock) remain open.

## 8. Conclusions

On the bi-objective CDP and GDP, a MOEA/D framework with a pool of eight operators and normalised objectives is significantly better than untuned NSGA-II, GRASP and VNS baselines, at sizes up to 500 and under three quality indicators. It stays ahead of tuned NSGA-II, SMS-EMOA and GRASP and of a tabu search and a VNS in the style of Colmenar et al. when all methods are tuned with the same protocol and run for the same wall-clock time — clearly on the GDP, and on the CDP up to the point where five times the time lets tuned GRASP, VNS and tabu search catch up. On the unconstrained MDP the order reverses: the tabu search, the VNS and a tuned GRASP are better than MOEA/D, which falls short at both ends of the front and does not improve with five times the time on the small instances.

Adaptive operator selection does not improve on uniform selection from the pool on instances up to about 200 elements nor on the CDP, whatever the selector (UCB1, Thompson sampling, the published DYTS, FRRMAB), reward, hyper-parameters, budget or context, and the scalar-reward selectors are significantly worse. The HV-reward selectors do win on large instances (ten of ten MDG-b instances with 2,000 elements) and on tightly constrained ones (GDP with the 0.2 cost factor), with an advantage that grows as the budget shrinks; there a third of the children need repair and the search is far from convergence at the standard budget. The pool needs MaxMinSwap, is robust to the removal of its three costly or redundant operators and tolerates three deliberately useless ones. A short MaxMin intensification brings the front's MaxMin end to within about 1% of the best published CDP values on four of five instance families (0.99 on GKD-c; 0.85 on the hardest, MDG-b), and above the published GDP values. In the CDP and GDP the size of the set is a hidden dimension of the bi-objective problem, which all methods leave at the size induced by the capacity constraint. We release data and code to let others test selection policies and baselines against the uniform pool we propose.
