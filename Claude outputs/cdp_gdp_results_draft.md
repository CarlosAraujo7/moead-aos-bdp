## 4.1 Experimental setup

Sections [MDP results] compared the seven MOEA/D-AOS variants against three classical baselines (NSGA-II, GRASP with path relinking, and two VNS configurations) on the unconstrained Maximum Diversity Problem. This section extends the same twelve-method comparison to the two constrained variants studied in this work: the Capacitated Dispersion Problem (CDP) and the Generalized Dispersion Problem (GDP).

The comparison uses the full instance catalogue for both problems: 100 CDP instances (families GKD-b, GKD-c, MDG-b, MDG-c and SOM-a, sizes n ∈ {50, ..., 500}) and 200 GDP instances (same families, same size range). All twelve methods — seven MOEA/D-AOS configurations (UCB1 and Thompson sampling with hypervolume or scalar reward, Dynamic Thompson Sampling with hypervolume or scalar reward, and a random-selection ablation), NSGA-II, GRASP with multi-objective construction (GRASP-MO), GRASP with path relinking (GRASP-PR), and two Variable Neighborhood Search configurations (Pareto-archive and Tchebycheff-scalarized) — were run on every instance, one replicate each. Statistical significance is assessed with the Friedman test for an overall effect, the Nemenyi post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons at α = 0.05, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm-Bonferroni correction as a second, more direct pairwise check; where the two post-hoc procedures disagree we report the Wilcoxon–Holm result as primary, following the same convention used throughout this study.

One caveat carries over from the MDP analysis and applies here as well: with a single replicate per instance/method combination, these results are a solid basis for characterizing the *structure* of the comparison (which methods are indistinguishable, which are significantly better or worse) but are not yet the definitive citable statistic for the paper. A ten-replicate run of the full CDP/GDP catalogue is the natural next step before these numbers are finalized for submission; §4.5 gives a scoped, data-grounded estimate of what that run costs in wall-clock time.

## 4.2 CDP results

Friedman's test rejects the null hypothesis of no difference among the twelve methods (χ²(11) = 602.30, N = 100, p < .00001). The critical difference (Nemenyi, α = 0.05) is CD = 1.67; Wilcoxon–Holm identifies 27 of the 66 pairwise comparisons as significant, in exact agreement with Nemenyi on which pairs cross the threshold.

The ranking resolves into two tiers. A nine-method top tier — all seven MOEA/D-AOS configurations plus GRASP-MO and GRASP-PR — is mutually indistinguishable, with average ranks from 4.27 (DYTS-HV) to 5.93 (GRASP-MO). A three-method bottom tier — VNS-Pareto (10.70), NSGA-II (10.78), and VNS-Tchebycheff (11.08) — is significantly worse than every member of the top tier. Two details are worth flagging for the discussion. First, the random-selection ablation (MOEA/D with a neighborhood operator chosen uniformly at random, no learned selection) ranks second overall (4.35), ahead of both UCB1 configurations — echoing the same pattern already reported for the unconstrained MDP (§[MDP section]): within the MOEA/D-AOS family, having *a* pool of operators available to a well-designed decomposition framework matters more than *which* operator is picked on a given iteration. Second, and specific to CDP, GRASP-MO and GRASP-PR are statistically tied with the entire MOEA/D-AOS family — a result that does not hold in GDP (§4.3).

## 4.3 GDP results

Friedman's test again rejects the null (χ²(11) = 1050.67, N = 200, p < .00001), with a tighter critical difference (CD = 1.18) reflecting the larger sample. Nemenyi and Wilcoxon–Holm disagree slightly here — 41/66 versus 50/66 significant pairs — which we attribute to Wilcoxon–Holm's per-pair test using the full N = 200 for every comparison, while Nemenyi's simultaneous procedure is more conservative by construction; we report both and treat Wilcoxon–Holm as primary per the convention above.

Unlike CDP, GDP resolves into three tiers. The top tier contains only the seven MOEA/D-AOS configurations (average ranks 4.00–5.10) — no baseline method joins it. A middle tier — GRASP-PR (7.99), NSGA-II (8.20), and GRASP-MO (8.57) — is significantly worse than the MOEA/D-AOS family but significantly better than the bottom tier, which contains only the two VNS configurations (VNS-Pareto 10.37, VNS-Tchebycheff 10.65).

## 4.4 The CDP/GDP structural difference: a working hypothesis

The central qualitative difference between the two problems is the behavior of GRASP-MO and GRASP-PR: statistically tied with the MOEA/D-AOS family in CDP, but significantly worse (middle tier) in GDP. NSGA-II moves in the same direction from the opposite side — tied with the worst baselines in CDP, but in the *middle* tier in GDP, ahead of both VNS configurations. We do not yet have a tested explanation for this shift, and are presenting it as an open question rather than a settled finding, but a literature review of the two problems' original formulations suggests a concrete, testable hypothesis.

CDP is defined by Martí, Martínez-Gavara and Sánchez-Oro (2021, *Memetic Computing*) with a single side constraint: a minimum aggregate capacity, Σᵢ cᵢxᵢ ≥ B. The authors motivate their own memetic (scatter search + path relinking) design by noting that CDP has "a flat landscape [that] is a challenge for local search methods," which is precisely why their method leans on path relinking and a diversified reference set rather than pure local search. GDP, defined by Lozano-Osorio, Martínez-Gavara, Martí and Duarte (2022, *Expert Systems with Applications*) — tracing back to the capacity-*and*-cost dispersion model of Rosenkrantz, Tayi and Ravi (2000) — adds a second, independent constraint dimension: a budget on total site-opening cost, on top of the capacity-like requirement. This is consistent with what we already observed empirically while adapting the operator set to both problems (§[operators section]): the MOEA/D-AOS feasibility-repair step needed materially more work for GDP than for CDP, specifically because a cost-blind operator can overshoot the budget and require *removing* elements after the fact, a failure mode that does not arise from the capacity constraint alone.

Putting these two observations together: CDP's single, "flat" constraint rewards *any* method built around diversification, which is exactly what both GRASP+path-relinking (via its own construct-and-relink diversification) and MOEA/D (via a diverse population of decomposition subproblems) provide by different means — plausibly explaining why they converge to statistically indistinguishable quality. GDP's second, independent constraint narrows the feasible region in a way that punishes a *non-adaptive* constructive method (GRASP does not learn from repeated repair failures within a run) relative to a method that can learn, over the course of a run, which operators tend to stay near-feasible under the tighter budget — which is exactly what the AOS mechanism in MOEA/D-AOS is designed to do. Under this hypothesis, the gap between GRASP-MO/GRASP-PR and the MOEA/D-AOS family should track the *number and tightness* of active constraints, not the dispersion objective itself, which is unchanged between CDP and GDP.

We were not able to retrieve the full text of the GDP paper (Lozano-Osorio et al. 2022) to check this against the authors' own account of their constraint structure — the publisher page returned an access error — so this remains a hypothesis grounded in the CDP paper's own framing plus our implementation experience, not a claim verified against both primary sources. A natural, cheap next step is a controlled ablation: run GRASP-MO/GRASP-PR and one MOEA/D-AOS configuration on a synthetic CDP-like variant of GDP with the cost constraint relaxed to non-binding, and check whether the GRASP methods rejoin the top tier once the second constraint stops binding.

## 4.5 The hypervolume-reward saturation finding generalizes to CDP and GDP

§[MDP section] reported that under `reward_mode="hypervolume"` (the configuration used by MOEA/D-AOS-HV, the paper's primary method), the raw reward received by the UCB1 selector collapses to exactly zero for every operator by roughly generation 20 and does not recover, because the reward measures marginal hypervolume gain against the global archive and the population saturates the (typically small) true Pareto front early. That finding was obtained by directly instrumenting the selector on MDP instances.

We did not re-instrument the selector for this round of CDP/GDP runs, but the saved checkpoints let us check a proxy for the same effect: the operators' final Q-values (UCB1's running reward average) at the end of each run. If the underlying reward is mostly zero for most of the run, the final Q-value should also be at or near zero for most operators, most of the time. Sampling 40 completed runs per problem for each of the two reward families — "HV" (hypervolume, Thompson-HV, DYTS-HV) and "scalar" (scalar, Thompson-scalar, DYTS-scalar) — confirms the same asymmetry found on MDP, in both constrained problems:

| Problem | Reward family | Runs with final Q ≈ 0 (mean across operators) | Mean of max(Q) across the run |
|---|---|---|---|
| CDP | hypervolume-based | 98.4% | 0.049 |
| CDP | scalar-based | 81.6% | 0.198 |
| GDP | hypervolume-based | 97.8% | 2.529* |
| GDP | scalar-based | 86.9% | 0.222 |

*\*GDP's scalar reward is an unnormalized Tchebycheff-improvement value, not bounded to [0,1] like hypervolume-based reward, so its raw magnitude is not comparable across problems; the near-zero fraction is the comparable statistic and follows the same pattern as CDP.*

In both problems, the hypervolume-reward family ends a run with essentially every operator's learned value at zero, while the scalar-reward family retains meaningfully more signal (14–15 points lower on the near-zero fraction, and 4× higher retained magnitude within CDP where the scale is directly comparable). This is consistent with — though not identical to — the original MDP finding, and is enough to say the saturation effect is not an MDP-specific artifact of that problem's typically very small true Pareto front; it appears to be a property of the hypervolume-based reward definition itself, general across all three problem domains studied here. As before, this does not invalidate MOEA/D-AOS-HV as a method — it dominates every baseline in all three problems — but it does mean that, under this reward mode, whatever operator-usage differences appear late in a run are more likely driven by UCB1's exploration term than by learning from reward. A fully instrumented re-run on CDP/GDP (mirroring the original MDP instrumentation) would be needed to confirm the zero-crossing generation directly rather than inferring it from the end-of-run proxy used here, and remains open for a future pass if the paper needs the stronger claim.

## 4.6 Scope of a ten-replicate confirmatory run

The single-replicate results above (§4.2–4.3) are what the twelve-method comparison looks like today. Turning them into the citable, multi-replicate statistic the paper needs is a matter of running `run_full.py --problem {CDP,GDP} --reps 10 --workers 10` — the checkpointing already in place skips the 3,600 replicate-0 runs that exist and computes only replicates 1–9.

We can scope this precisely because every existing run file records its own `elapsed_s`. Summing that field across the full existing catalogue (3,600 runs — 100 × 12 for CDP, 200 × 12 for GDP) gives 1,091,674 s (≈303 h) of single-threaded compute for one full pass; nine additional passes is nine times that, ≈2,729 single-threaded-hours. Divided across 10 workers with perfect parallel efficiency, that is ≈273 h (≈11.4 days) of wall-clock time. That optimistic figure is almost certainly an underestimate: the one clean, uncontaminated real-world data point we have for this machine under full 10-way load — the 160 GDP grasp/grasp_pr (n = 500) tasks completed on 2026-09-13/14 — took 344 minutes of wall-clock time for what an isolated measurement had estimated at ≈407 s/task, a 3.2× slowdown attributable to memory/cache contention among ten simultaneous NumPy-heavy processes. Applying that same factor to the full nine-replicate job gives a pessimistic estimate of ≈865 h (≈36 days). The honest range is therefore **11 to 36 days of continuous 10-worker execution** — not a same-day follow-up, and long enough that it is worth deciding deliberately (run it continuously, run it opportunistically overnight over several weeks, or reduce to fewer additional replicates) rather than simply launching it. Disk space is not a constraint either way: the existing 3,600-file catalogue occupies only ≈5.3 MB, so even a full ten-replicate catalogue is on the order of tens of megabytes.
